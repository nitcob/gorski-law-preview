Oregon has four degrees of assault. The degree depends mostly on how badly the person was hurt, whether a weapon was involved, what mental state the state can prove and, in some cases, who the alleged victim was. Assault IV is usually a Class A misdemeanor. Assault III is a Class C felony. Assault II and Assault I are felonies with mandatory minimum prison terms under the law known as Measure 11.

## How does Oregon grade assault?

The table gives examples of what each degree covers. Each statute lists more situations than fit here.

| Degree                                                                                   | Examples of what the state must prove                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Class                                                    | Mandatory minimum |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Assault IV ([ORS 163.160](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors163.html)) | Intentionally, knowingly or recklessly causing physical injury; or, through criminal negligence, causing physical injury with a deadly weapon                                                                                                                                                | Class A misdemeanor; Class C felony in listed situations | None              |
| Assault III ([ORS 163.165](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.165))              | Recklessly causing serious physical injury with a deadly or dangerous weapon; intentionally or knowingly causing physical injury while aided by another person who is present; an adult intentionally or knowingly injuring a child 10 or younger; injuring an on-duty transit operator, taxi driver, emergency medical provider or highway worker | Class C felony; Class B in some DUII crashes             | None              |
| Assault II ([ORS 163.175](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.175))               | Intentionally or knowingly causing serious physical injury; or intentionally or knowingly causing physical injury with a deadly or dangerous weapon                                                                                                                                          | Class B felony                                           | 70 months         |
| Assault I ([ORS 163.185](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.185))                | Intentionally causing serious physical injury with a deadly or dangerous weapon; or intentionally or knowingly causing serious physical injury to a child under 6                                                                                                                            | Class A felony                                           | 90 months         |

Mandatory minimums: [ORS 137.700](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_137.700).

## What do "physical injury" and "dangerous weapon" mean?

The degrees are built from a few defined terms, so how the injury and any object are described can change the charge ([ORS 161.015](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.015)).

- **Physical injury** means impairment of physical condition or substantial pain.
- **Serious physical injury** means an injury that creates a substantial risk of death, or causes serious and protracted disfigurement, protracted impairment of health, or protracted loss or impairment of an organ's function.
- **Deadly weapon** means something specifically designed for and presently capable of causing death or serious physical injury.
- **Dangerous weapon** means any weapon, device, instrument, material or substance that, in the way it is used, attempted to be used or threatened to be used, is readily capable of causing death or serious physical injury. An everyday object can qualify depending on how it was used.

## When is Assault IV a felony?

Assault IV under the injury or weapon parts of the statute becomes a Class C felony when any of these is true ([ORS 163.160](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors163.html)):

- The assault happens in the immediate presence of, or is seen or otherwise directly perceived by, a minor child of either person or a minor child living in the household.
- The person has a prior conviction for assault, strangulation or menacing involving the same victim.
- The person has at least three prior convictions for assault, strangulation or menacing, in any combination.
- The person knew the victim was pregnant.
- The victim was performing official duties at work, and the person has two or more prior assault convictions, at least two of them against victims who were working. This last situation was added by [SB 170 (2025)](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2025R1/Measures/Overview/SB170) and took effect January 1, 2026.

When an Assault IV charge involves family or household members, extra rules apply, covered on the [domestic violence charges page](/criminal-defense/domestic-violence/).

## What is Measure 11, and which assault charges does it cover?

Measure 11 is a ballot measure Oregon voters passed in November 1994. It is now part of ORS chapter 137 and requires mandatory minimum prison sentences for listed crimes against persons ([Oregon Legislature background brief](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/lpro/Publications/Measure11.pdf)).

For a conviction on Assault II in Oregon, Measure 11 sets a 70-month minimum. For Assault I it is 90 months. The court must impose at least that term, and the person must serve all of it, without eligibility for any sentence reduction ([ORS 137.700](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_137.700)).

There is one narrow exception for assault. When Assault II is charged under the weapon part of the statute, a judge may impose a lower sentence only after making specific findings on the record, including that the victim was not physically injured by means of a deadly weapon and did not suffer a significant physical injury, and that there is a substantial and compelling reason ([ORS 137.712](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_137.712)).

Assault III in Oregon is not a Measure 11 crime. That is one reason the exact degree charged matters.

## Can self-defense apply to an assault charge?

It can. Oregon law allows a person to use the degree of force they reasonably believe is necessary to defend themselves or someone else from what they reasonably believe is the use or imminent use of unlawful force ([ORS 161.209](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.209)). When self-defense is properly raised at trial, the state must disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt ([ORS 161.055](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.055)).

Other questions can matter too: whether the injury meets the legal definition, what mental state the evidence shows and whether an object was used in a way that makes it a dangerous weapon.

## What else can an assault conviction bring?

Beyond jail or prison, a court that finds a crime caused economic damages, such as medical bills, generally must order restitution for the full amount ([ORS 137.106](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors137.html)). Menacing is a related charge. It means intentionally trying to place someone in fear of imminent serious physical injury by word or conduct, and it is a Class A misdemeanor ([ORS 163.190](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.190)).
