An Oregon criminal charge is either a misdemeanor or a felony, and its class sets the most a court can impose. A Class A misdemeanor carries up to 364 days in jail; a Class A felony carries up to 20 years in prison ([ORS 161.615](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.615), [ORS 161.605](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.605)). Gorski Law defends people charged with Oregon crimes, from misdemeanors to felonies, including domestic violence, assault, theft, drug charges and DUII.

## Misdemeanor vs felony in Oregon: how are crimes classified?

Oregon law sorts every offense into one of two groups. A crime is an offense that can be punished with jail or prison. A violation is not a crime ([ORS 161.505](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.505), [ORS 161.515](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.515)).

Crimes are either felonies or misdemeanors. A felony can be punished by more than one year of imprisonment. A misdemeanor can be punished by no more than one year ([ORS 161.525](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.525), [ORS 161.545](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.545)). Each group is split into Class A, B and C, plus "unclassified" crimes whose penalties are set in their own statute ([ORS 161.535](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.535), [ORS 161.555](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.555)).

## What are the maximum penalties for each class?

The table shows the statutory maximums for each class and an example of a charge in that class.

| Class               | Maximum jail or prison | Maximum fine | Example charge                                                                           |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Class A felony      | 20 years               | $375,000     | Assault I ([ORS 163.185](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.185))                |
| Class B felony      | 10 years               | $250,000     | Assault II ([ORS 163.175](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.175))               |
| Class C felony      | 5 years                | $125,000     | Theft I ([ORS 164.055](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_164.055))                  |
| Class A misdemeanor | 364 days               | $6,250       | Assault IV ([ORS 163.160](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors163.html)) |
| Class B misdemeanor | 6 months               | $2,500       | Harassment ([ORS 166.065](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors166.html)) |
| Class C misdemeanor | 30 days                | $1,250       | Theft III ([ORS 164.043](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_164.043))                |

Sources: prison terms, [ORS 161.605](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.605) and [ORS 161.615](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.615); fines, [ORS 161.625](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.625) and [ORS 161.635](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.635).

These are ceilings, not typical sentences. Felony sentences are generally set by the sentencing guidelines adopted by the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission ([ORS 137.669](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_137.669)). Some serious felonies, including Assault I and Assault II, carry mandatory minimum prison terms instead ([ORS 137.700](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_137.700)). The same charge can also move up a class because of the facts: Assault IV, for example, becomes a felony in certain circumstances.

> **Note:** A Class C felony is not always entered as a felony. A judge may enter the conviction as a Class A misdemeanor if the judge believes a felony conviction would be unduly harsh, considering the crime and the person's history ([ORS 161.705](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.705)). That is the judge's call, not a right.

## What happens after an arrest or citation in Oregon?

Oregon criminal cases generally move through these early steps, whatever the charge.

1. **Arrest or citation.** Some people are taken into custody. Others get a criminal citation with a court date, which generally can be no later than 30 days after the citation was issued. If you miss that date and a charge is filed, the court must issue an arrest warrant when one is requested ([ORS 133.060](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors133.html)).
2. **Arraignment.** If you are held in custody, the arraignment generally must take place within 36 hours, not counting Saturdays, Sundays and holidays ([ORS 135.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.010)). If you arrive without a lawyer, the court must tell you that you have the right to one ([ORS 135.040](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.040)).
3. **Release conditions.** A person released before trial agrees to appear in court, follow the court's orders and not leave Oregon without the court's permission. The court can add other conditions. In a domestic violence case, no contact with the alleged victim must be a condition unless the court waives it after a hearing ([ORS 135.250](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.250)).
4. **The prosecution.** The district attorney brings the case on behalf of the state ([ORS 8.660](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_8.660)). The person who called the police is not the party bringing the charge.
5. **Trial, if there is one.** You have the right to a public trial by an impartial jury ([ORS 136.001](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_136.001)). You are presumed innocent, and if there is a reasonable doubt about guilt, you are entitled to be acquitted ([ORS 136.415](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_136.415)).

A case can also end without a trial: through a plea agreement, a diversion or conditional discharge program where the law allows one, or a dismissal. Which options exist depends on the charge, your history and the facts.

## What should you keep in mind in the first days?

These steps are widely accepted, whatever the charge.

- Write down your court date and be there. Missing court can lead to a warrant.
- Read your release agreement and follow every condition, including any no-contact condition.
- Keep every paper you were given: the citation, the release agreement and any property receipt.
- Talk to a lawyer before your first court date if you can.
