An Oregon domestic violence charge is usually an ordinary crime, such as Assault IV, menacing, harassment or strangulation, with the words "constituting domestic violence" added to its title ([ORS 132.586](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_132.586)). Domestic violence means abuse between family or household members ([ORS 135.230](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.230)). The label matters because it brings mandatory arrest, a required no-contact condition and, after some convictions, firearm restrictions.

## Who counts as a family or household member?

For charging and release decisions, Oregon law counts these relationships ([ORS 135.230](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.230)):

- Spouses and former spouses
- Adults related by blood or marriage
- People who live together, or who have lived together
- People who have been in a sexually intimate relationship
- Unmarried parents of a minor child

The arrest statute uses a similar list found in [ORS 107.705](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_107.705).

## Why did the police have to arrest someone?

In Oregon, some domestic calls require an arrest. When an officer responds to a domestic disturbance and has probable cause to believe an assault happened between family or household members, or that one put the other in fear of imminent serious physical injury, the officer must arrest the person believed to be the assailant ([ORS 133.055](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_133.055)). Strangulation counts as an assault for this rule.

The officer does not have to arrest both people. The statute tells officers to work out who the assailant is by weighing the injuries on each side, any history of domestic violence, whether either person acted in self-defense and the risk of future assaults.

## What does the no-contact condition mean?

Two Oregon laws keep the accused person away from the alleged victim while the case is open.

- **While in custody.** The release decision must include an order not to contact the alleged victim, directly or through a third party. At arraignment, the court continues or enters that order. It lasts until sentencing, dismissal or acquittal unless the court ends it on the alleged victim's petition after a hearing ([ORS 135.247](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.247)).
- **After release.** If the charge constitutes domestic violence, the release agreement must include a condition not to contact the alleged victim. The court may waive it only if the alleged victim petitions and the court finds, after a hearing, that waiving it is in the best interests of the parties and the community ([ORS 135.250](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.250)). The agreement can also include terms that affect firearm possession.

> **Warning:** The no-contact condition has no exception for calls, texts or visits the other person starts. Police must arrest someone they have probable cause to believe broke a no-contact release condition ([ORS 133.310](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_133.310)).

For a step-by-step look at living under these conditions, read [what a no-contact order means in an Oregon domestic violence case](/blog/domestic-violence-charges-oregon-no-contact-order/).

## Which charges can carry the domestic violence label?

Several Oregon crimes can carry the domestic violence label, including Assault IV, strangulation, menacing and harassment. The table shows each charge's usual class and what can make it more serious.

| Charge                                                                                   | Usual class         | When it is more serious                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assault IV ([ORS 163.160](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors163.html)) | Class A misdemeanor | Class C felony in listed situations, including when a minor child of either person, or one living in the household, sees or otherwise directly perceives it; when there is a prior assault, strangulation or menacing conviction involving the same victim; or when the person knew the victim was pregnant |
| Strangulation ([ORS 163.187](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.187))            | Class A misdemeanor | Class C felony if the victim is a family or household member, among other situations                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Menacing ([ORS 163.190](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_163.190))                 | Class A misdemeanor | No separate felony version                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| Harassment ([ORS 166.065](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors166.html)) | Class B misdemeanor | Class A misdemeanor in listed situations, including offensive physical contact with a family or household member in the presence of, or witnessed by, a minor child of either person or of the household                                                                                                                                                                 |

Because strangulation of a family or household member is a Class C felony, a strangulation allegation can turn a domestic case into a felony case. When a misdemeanor Assault IV or a menacing conviction is pleaded as domestic violence, the judgment must state that it constitutes domestic violence (ORS 163.160, ORS 163.190). For how Oregon grades assault more broadly, see [Oregon assault charges from Assault IV to Assault I](/criminal-defense/assault/).

## Can a domestic violence conviction affect gun rights?

Yes. Under Oregon law, it is unlawful to knowingly possess a firearm or ammunition after a conviction for a "qualifying misdemeanor" when the person was a family or household member, parent or guardian of the victim. A qualifying misdemeanor is one that has the use or attempted use of physical force, or the threatened use of a deadly weapon, as an element ([ORS 166.255](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_166.255)). The same statute covers some restraining orders.

Federal law separately makes it unlawful for anyone convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence to possess a firearm or ammunition ([18 U.S.C. 922(g)(9)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/922)).

## What if I was defending myself?

Oregon law allows a person to use the degree of force they reasonably believe is necessary to defend against what they reasonably believe is the use or imminent use of unlawful force ([ORS 161.209](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.209)). When self-defense is properly raised at trial, the state must disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt ([ORS 161.055](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.055)). Whether it applies depends on the facts, and a lawyer can help you sort out which ones matter.
