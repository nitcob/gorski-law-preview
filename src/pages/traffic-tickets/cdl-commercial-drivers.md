For a CDL holder in Oregon, a traffic ticket can cost far more than the fine. Paying it counts as a conviction. Federal and Oregon law bar courts from hiding it through deferral or diversion. And some convictions, including some in your own car, suspend your commercial driving privileges for 60 days, a year or life. If you drive a truck or a school bus for a living, deciding how to answer a ticket is really a decision about your job.

## Who counts as a commercial driver in Oregon?

Oregon treats you as holding commercial driving privileges if you have an unexpired commercial learner permit (CLP), or if your most recently issued license is or was a CDL, even if it has expired or is suspended ([ORS 807.018](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_807.018)).

A commercial motor vehicle is, generally, one rated at 26,001 pounds or more (alone or in combination), one designed to carry 16 or more people including the driver, or a vehicle of any size hauling hazardous materials ([ORS 801.208](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.208)). The statute has more detail and some exceptions, such as a motor home used for personal travel.

Federal rules call the loss of a CDL a "disqualification." Oregon law calls it a suspension of commercial driving privileges. This page uses Oregon's word.

## Why does a ticket in my own car count against my CDL?

Because the rules follow the driver, not the vehicle.

- **Convictions count in any vehicle.** Federal rules make a CDL holder subject to disqualification for listed convictions in a commercial vehicle or a personal one. Each listed conviction from a separate incident counts, whatever you were driving ([49 CFR 383.51](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-383.51)).
- **No masking.** A state may not mask a CDL holder's conviction, defer judgment, or use diversion to keep a traffic conviction in any type of vehicle off the driver record. Parking, vehicle weight and vehicle defect violations are the only exceptions ([49 CFR 384.226](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-384.226)). Oregon writes the same rule into its own law ([ORS 153.090](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.090)), and [Oregon DMV](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/dmv/pages/driverid/federal_cdl_reqs.aspx) says CDL and CLP holders are not eligible for any diversion for traffic offenses.
- **Paying is a conviction.** Under federal rules, paying a fine or court cost counts as a conviction ([49 CFR 383.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-383.5)). The Oregon Judicial Department says only a not-guilty verdict or a dismissal by a judge keeps a violation off your record ([Traffic Options](https://www.courts.oregon.gov/services/online/Pages/Traffic-Options.aspx)).
- **Your employer will hear about it.** You must tell your employer in writing within 30 days of being convicted of any traffic violation except parking, in any type of vehicle ([49 CFR 383.31](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-383.31)). You must report a suspension by the end of the next business day ([49 CFR 383.33](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-383.33)). Motor carriers pull each driver's record at least once a year and must give "great weight" to violations such as speeding and reckless driving ([49 CFR 391.25](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-391.25)).
- **A missed court date counts too.** Oregon DMV suspends commercial privileges if a CDL holder fails to appear in court or fails to pay a traffic fine in any state.

## What is a "serious traffic offense"?

Oregon DMV designates serious traffic offenses by rule, matched to federal rules as far as practicable ([ORS 809.525](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_809.525)). According to [Oregon DMV](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/dmv/pages/driverid/federal_cdl_reqs.aspx), the list depends on what you were driving.

**In any vehicle, while you hold a CDL or CLP:**

- Reckless driving.
- Speeding at 100 mph or more.
- Speeding 30 mph or more over the limit, when the court orders a suspension.

**In a commercial motor vehicle:**

- Driving 15 mph or more over the posted limit.
- Following too closely, failing to drive within a lane, or unsafe passing.
- Driving on the left on a curve or grade, or at an intersection or rail crossing.
- Driving while using a mobile electronic device.
- Driving without driving privileges, or failing to carry or show proof of your CDL.
- Any traffic-control violation connected to a fatal crash.

So an ordinary speeding ticket in your own car, say 12 mph over, is not on the serious list. It still goes on your record, you still have to report it to your employer, and your carrier will see it at the annual review. A CDL speeding ticket Oregon officers write while you are in the truck is a different matter: at 15 mph over, it is a serious offense.

Trucks and buses also have their own limits. Motor trucks rated over 10,000 pounds, most truck tractors and school buses are limited to 55 mph on most highways unless a different speed is posted, with separate, lower truck limits on some faster highways east of the Cascades ([ORS 811.111](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_811.111)).

## How do DUII and breath tests affect a CDL?

DUII is Oregon's legal term for what most states call DUI: driving under the influence of intoxicants. Oregon's criminal DUII law uses 0.08 percent for every driver ([ORS 813.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.010)). The CDL rules reach further.

- **0.04 in a commercial vehicle.** Under the implied consent law, a driver of a commercial motor vehicle fails a breath or blood test at 0.04 percent or more ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410)). DMV then suspends commercial privileges for one year, or three years if hauling hazardous materials ([ORS 809.510](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_809.510)).
- **Your own car still counts.** A CDL holder who tests at 0.08 or more in any motor vehicle loses commercial privileges for one year. Refusing a test in any motor vehicle means three years.
- **No DUII diversion.** A person who held commercial privileges on the date of the offense, or was driving a commercial vehicle, is not eligible for Oregon's DUII diversion program ([ORS 813.215](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.215)). See our page on [Oregon DUII diversion](/duii-defense/diversion/) for how the program works for other drivers.
- **No hardship permit for a truck.** DMV may not issue a hardship permit that authorizes driving a commercial motor vehicle ([ORS 807.240](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_807.240)).

> **Deadline:** After an implied consent arrest, you usually have 10 days from the date of arrest to request a DMV hearing (in blood-test cases, 10 days from when DMV sends notice). A hearing is also available on the proposed CDL suspension ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410); [ORS 809.545](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_809.545)). Read more about the [Oregon implied consent hearing](/duii-defense/dmv-implied-consent-hearing/).

## What can a lawyer do with a CDL ticket?

A commercial driver ticket Oregon courts hear goes through the same violation process as any other ticket. Because masking is banned, though, the real question is usually whether a conviction happens at all, and for which offense. Your options depend on the facts, but they can include:

- Checking whether the charged offense is on the serious list, and whether the citation and evidence support it.
- Counting your record: which convictions fall inside the three-year window, and what one more would trigger.
- Requesting the state's evidence through discovery and preparing for trial, where the state must prove a violation by a preponderance of the evidence before a judge ([ORS 153.076](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.076)).
- Requesting and preparing for a DMV hearing when a breath test or refusal is involved.

The goal is to avoid a conviction that triggers a suspension. No lawyer can promise that.
