DUI vs DUII is a naming difference, not a different crime. DUII is Oregon's legal term for what most states call DUI: driving under the influence of intoxicants. Oregon's statute, [ORS 813.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.010), names the offense "driving while under the influence of intoxicants," and that last word is where the extra "I" comes from.

The word matters because Oregon defines "intoxicant" broadly. It covers alcohol, but also cannabis, controlled substances, inhalants, psilocybin and other drugs that impair. This post explains what the term means, how the 0.08 rule fits in, and what stays the same whatever you call it. It is general information, not legal advice.

## What does the extra "I" stand for?

It stands for "intoxicants." Under ORS 813.010, a person commits DUII by driving a vehicle while any one of these is true:

1. A breath or blood test shows 0.08 percent or more by weight of alcohol in the blood.
2. The person is under the influence of an intoxicant, or a combination of intoxicants.
3. Within two hours after driving, without drinking in between, a breath or blood test shows 0.08 percent or more.

The first and third are number-based. The second is not tied to a number at all. It asks whether the person was under the influence of an intoxicant, and that is why the definition of "intoxicant" matters so much in Oregon.

## What counts as an "intoxicant" in Oregon?

Oregon's vehicle code defines the word in [ORS 801.321](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.321). Here is the list in plain terms:

| Category              | What the law includes                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Intoxicating liquor   | Alcohol                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| Controlled substance  | Drugs regulated as controlled substances                                                                                                                                                                           |
| Inhalant              | Glue, paint, cement or another substance capable of causing intoxication that contains a listed chemical compound, such as butane or nitrous oxide ([ORS 801.317](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.317)) |
| Cannabis              | Marijuana                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| Psilocybin            | Psilocybin                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Other impairing drugs | Any drug that, alone or combined with the items above, adversely affects a person's mental or physical faculties to a noticeable or perceptible degree                                                             |

This definition came from [House Bill 2316](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2023R1/Measures/Overview/HB2316), passed in the 2023 session. The Legislature's summary says the bill expanded the DUII offense to cover any substance that can impair a person's ability to drive safely, defined "intoxicant" for the vehicle code and created an affirmative defense.

That defense is narrow. It applies only to the "other impairing drugs" category, never to alcohol, a controlled substance, an inhalant, cannabis or psilocybin. It covers a person who took a prescription or over-the-counter drug as directed and had a reaction they could not reasonably have anticipated. The defense requires written notice before trial, generally at least 45 days before the first trial date ([ORS 813.015](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.015)).

There is also a charging rule. A person cannot be convicted of DUII based on a controlled substance or an inhalant unless that is pleaded in the charging document and either proved at trial or admitted in a guilty plea ([ORS 813.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.010)). Drug and cannabis cases raise their own proof questions; our post on [how marijuana and drug impairment is proven in Oregon](/blog/marijuana-drug-duii-oregon-how-impairment-is-proven/) covers them.

## Is Oregon's legal limit still 0.08?

Yes. Not less than 0.08 percent by weight of alcohol in the blood constitutes being under the influence of intoxicating liquor ([ORS 813.300](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.300)). The percentage is measured as grams of alcohol per 100 milliliters of blood or per 210 liters of breath.

A lower number does not end the question. Under ORS 813.300, a result below 0.08 is indirect evidence that can be used with other evidence to decide whether a person was under the influence. And because ORS 813.010 also covers being "under the influence of an intoxicant," a DUII charge does not depend on a breath number at all when drugs or cannabis are alleged.

Different numbers apply on the DMV side. For the implied consent suspension, a test fails at 0.04 percent if you were driving a commercial motor vehicle, and at any amount if you are under 21 ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410)).

| Situation                                          | Alcohol level that matters                  | Source                        |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| DUII crime, any adult driver                       | 0.08% or more                               | ORS 813.010(1)(a), 813.300    |
| DMV suspension, not in a commercial motor vehicle  | 0.08% or more                               | ORS 813.410(8)(c)             |
| DMV suspension, driving a commercial motor vehicle | 0.04% or more                               | ORS 813.410(8)(c)             |
| DMV suspension, driver under 21                    | Any amount                                  | ORS 813.300(3), 813.410(8)(c) |
| Below 0.08%                                        | Indirect evidence, used with other evidence | ORS 813.300(1)                |

## Does the implied consent law cover drugs too?

Partly. The breath test under Oregon's implied consent law measures alcohol. A separate section covers urine. If you are arrested for DUII, you are treated as having agreed to a urine test for intoxicants other than alcohol in two situations: your breath test showed less than 0.08 percent, or you were involved in a crash that caused injury or property damage ([ORS 813.131](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.131)).

There are limits on that request. The officer must have at least eight hours of state-certified training in recognizing drug-impaired driving, and must reasonably suspect impairment from something other than alcohol. You must be given privacy while giving the sample. Our page on [marijuana and drug DUII in Oregon](/duii-defense/drug-and-marijuana-duii/) explains how those cases differ.

## Does DUII apply to more than cars?

The statute uses the word "vehicle," and Oregon defines a vehicle as any device in, upon or by which a person or property is or may be transported or drawn on a public highway ([ORS 801.590](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.590)). ORS 813.010 itself sets a separate minimum fine for a DUII committed while riding a bicycle, and says the offense applies on any premises open to the public, not only public roads.

## How serious is a DUII charge in Oregon?

A misdemeanor DUII is a Class A misdemeanor. It becomes a Class C felony if the current offense was in a motor vehicle and the person has at least three prior DUII convictions, or similar out-of-state convictions, in the 10 years before it ([ORS 813.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.010)). The same statute sets these fines:

- A minimum of $1,000 for a first conviction, $1,500 for a second and $2,000 for a third or later (if no term of imprisonment is imposed).
- A minimum of $2,000 if a test showed 0.15 percent or more.
- A maximum of $10,000 if a passenger under 18, and at least three years younger than the driver, was in the vehicle.

On conviction, the court must also order a screening interview and treatment, and either at least 48 hours of imprisonment or community service ([ORS 813.020](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.020)). DMV then separately suspends, or in some cases revokes, driving privileges because of the conviction ([ORS 813.400](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.400)). ## What stays the same, whatever you call it?

The label changes from state to state, but Oregon law treats the conduct as the same offense in several places:

- **Out-of-state DUI convictions count here.** Oregon counts another jurisdiction's DUI-type convictions toward felony DUII (ORS 813.010), toward the 15-year bar on diversion ([ORS 813.215](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.215)), and toward longer implied consent suspensions ([ORS 813.430](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.430)).
- **There are still two cases.** A breath test refusal or failure triggers a DMV suspension under the implied consent law ([ORS 813.100](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.100)), separate from the criminal charge. Our post on [what to do after a DUI arrest in Oregon](/blog/what-to-do-after-duii-arrest-oregon/) walks through both.
- **The paperwork says DUII.** Oregon statutes call the offense "driving while under the influence of intoxicants," and court forms, such as the [DUII diversion petition](https://www.courts.oregon.gov/forms/Documents/DUII_Diversion_Form_1_Petition_Agreement.pdf), shorten it to "DUII." If you search for "DUI," you are looking for the same thing.
