Refusing a breath test after a DUII arrest in Oregon leads to a one-year suspension of your driving privileges. It becomes three years if you are in a DUII diversion program now, or if in the five years before the arrest you were convicted of DUII, had an implied consent suspension take effect, or started diversion ([ORS 813.420](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.420), [ORS 813.430](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.430)). That is the basic Oregon breath test refusal suspension length, and it is longer than the suspension for taking the test and failing it.

The hardship permit rules follow the same split. This post walks through both, statute by statute, so you can see where each number comes from.

DUII is Oregon's legal term for what most states call DUI: driving under the influence of intoxicants ([ORS 813.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.010)).

## What is an implied consent suspension?

Oregon's implied consent law says that anyone who drives on Oregon highways, or on premises open to the public, is deemed to have consented to a breath test if arrested for DUII ([ORS 813.100](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.100)). Refusing the test, or taking it and showing 0.08 percent or more, leads to a DMV suspension.

That suspension runs separately from the criminal DUII case. The rights-and-consequences notice the officer reads says plainly that "the outcome of a criminal charge for driving under the influence of intoxicants will not affect the suspension" ([ORS 813.130](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.130)). Oregon DMV's own summary says the same thing ([Oregon DMV](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/DMV/pages/driverid/suspreasons.aspx)).

If you have a valid Oregon license, the officer takes it and issues a 30-day temporary driving permit (Oregon DMV). The suspension then starts on the 30th day after the arrest, unless you request a hearing and DMV decides the suspension is not valid ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410)).

## How long is the suspension for refusing versus failing?

ORS 813.420 sets four possible lengths. Which one applies depends on two things: whether you refused or failed, and whether anything in your history counts under ORS 813.430 (the "prior" rules explained in the next section).

| Your history under ORS 813.430 | Refused the breath test | Failed the breath test (0.08 or more) |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Nothing that counts            | 1 year                  | 90 days                               |
| Something that counts          | 3 years                 | 1 year                                |

Refusing a urine test that police request under the implied consent law is treated as a refusal of a breath test. That suspension runs consecutively, meaning it starts after any other implied consent suspension ends ([ORS 813.132](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.132)).

## What counts as a prior under the five-year rule?

ORS 813.430 lists what moves a refusal from one year to three. Any one of these is enough:

- **You are in diversion now.** You are currently in a DUII diversion program, or a similar alcohol or drug rehabilitation program, in Oregon or another state. This one has no five-year window. It is about the present.
- **Within the five years before the date of this arrest:**
  - an implied consent suspension under ORS 813.410 became effective;
  - you were convicted of DUII under Oregon law, a city ordinance, or a similar law in another state; or
  - you started a DUII diversion program or a similar program.

Two details are easy to miss. First, the five years are counted back from the date of the new arrest. Second, for a past implied consent suspension, the statute asks whether the suspension "became effective." An earlier arrest by itself is not on the list.

## Is there also a fine for refusing?

Yes. Refusing a breath test, or refusing a urine test requested under the implied consent law, is a separate offense. It is a "specific fine traffic violation" with a presumptive fine of $650, and that fine is "in addition to any other consequence prescribed by law" ([ORS 813.095](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.095)). The notice officers read describes it as a fine of at least $500 and not more than $1,000 (ORS 813.130).

The refusal itself can also be used as evidence. In DMV's words, "Refusal to take a test is admissible as evidence in court" ([Oregon DMV](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/DMV/pages/driverid/suspreasons.aspx)).

## When can you get a hardship permit after refusing?

A hardship permit lets you drive during set times for set reasons, such as work or medical care, while your license is suspended ([ORS 807.240](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_807.240)). After an implied consent suspension, [ORS 813.520](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.520) makes you wait before DMV may issue one.

| Suspension            | Length (ORS 813.420) | Earliest hardship permit (ORS 813.520)                      |
| --------------------- | -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Refusal, no prior     | 1 year               | 90 days after the suspension begins                         |
| Refusal, with a prior | 3 years              | 3 years after it begins, so no permit during the suspension |
| Failed, no prior      | 90 days              | 30 days after the suspension begins                         |
| Failed, with a prior  | 1 year               | 1 year after it begins, so no permit during the suspension  |

Three timing points:

- **The clock starts with the suspension, not the arrest.** Because a refusal suspension normally begins on the 30th day after arrest, the 90-day wait ends roughly four months after the arrest.
- **Consecutive suspensions double the wait.** If a urine test refusal is stacked on another implied consent suspension, the waiting period before a permit or reinstatement is doubled (ORS 813.132).
- **DMV will not issue the permit until the suspension is in effect** ([Oregon DMV: Hardship permits](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/DMV/Pages/DriverID/hardship.aspx)).

## Who qualifies for a hardship permit?

Waiting out the clock is only the first step. Under ORS 807.240, ORS 813.520 and DMV's hardship permit page, you generally need to:

- **Show a need to drive:** for your job, to get to work or look for work, to alcohol, drug or gambling treatment, for regular medical treatment, or for "necessary services" such as groceries, taking children to school or child care, medical appointments and caring for elderly family members.
- **Lack other transportation.** DMV may not issue a permit to someone who has public or private transportation that meets their needs (ORS 813.520).
- **Have an acceptable driving record.** You must not be an incompetent or habitually reckless driver on your record (ORS 807.240).
- **File proof of future responsibility.** This means an SR-22 certificate filed by your insurer.
- **Accept the limits.** The permit covers noncommercial vehicles only, at specified times, and DMV cannot allow more than 12 hours of driving on any one day.

## Will you need an ignition interlock device?

The interlock rules in [ORS 813.602](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.602) are tied to a DUII conviction or a DUII diversion agreement, not to the implied consent suspension itself. An ignition interlock device stops a vehicle from starting if it detects a breath alcohol concentration of 0.02 percent or more (Oregon DMV).

- **After a DUII conviction,** DMV must require an interlock before you are eligible for a hardship permit, for as long as the permit lasts, and then for one year after the suspension ends for a first conviction (two years for a second or later one).
- **In diversion,** if you refused a breath or blood test, the court must require an interlock in any vehicle you drive during the diversion agreement while you have driving privileges. A court may grant a medical exemption under DMV rules.

If diversion may be an option in your case, our post on [Oregon DUII diversion requirements](/blog/oregon-duii-diversion-requirements/) covers who qualifies.

## What if you hold a commercial driver license?

Refusal is harder on CDL holders. If you hold commercial driving privileges and refuse a test while driving any motor vehicle, DMV suspends your commercial privileges for three years. It is five years if you were driving a commercial motor vehicle carrying hazardous materials ([ORS 809.510](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_809.510)). A hardship permit cannot authorize driving a commercial motor vehicle (ORS 807.240). Our post on [how one ticket can cost a CDL driver their job](/blog/cdl-drivers-oregon-ticket-cost-your-job/) covers the wider federal rules.

## Can you challenge the suspension?

Yes. You can ask for a DMV hearing, but only if you ask in time. The hearing is narrow. It looks at questions such as whether you were under arrest for DUII, whether police had reasonable grounds, whether you refused, and whether you were told your rights and consequences and given written notice (ORS 813.410). If DMV finds the suspension is not valid, it does not take effect.

> **Deadline:** For a breath test refusal, DMV must receive your hearing request within 10 days of the date of arrest (ORS 813.410). Count from the arrest date.

Whether a hearing makes sense depends on your facts. We cover the pros and cons in [should you request an implied consent hearing?](/blog/should-you-request-implied-consent-hearing/)

## What can you do this week?

- Write down the arrest date and the date 10 days later.
- Keep the written notice of intent to suspend the officer gave you.
- List anything from the past five years that might count under ORS 813.430.
- Do not drive after the temporary permit ends unless you have a valid permit to do so.
- Talk to a lawyer about both the DMV hearing and the criminal case, since they run on separate tracks.
