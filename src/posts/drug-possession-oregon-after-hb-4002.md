Since September 1, 2024, possessing a small amount of a drug such as meth, heroin, cocaine or fentanyl in Oregon has been a crime again. Under the Oregon drug possession law HB 4002 created, it is a "drug enforcement misdemeanor," and the usual sentence is probation, not jail. Before that date, under Measure 110, most small-amount possession was a Class E violation with a maximum fine of $100.

HB 4002 also created two paths that can end without a conviction: deflection and a new form of conditional discharge. Which rules apply depends on when the alleged possession happened, what the drug was and how much there was.

## What did Measure 110 do, and what did HB 4002 change?

In November 2020, Oregon voters passed Ballot Measure 110, which reduced penalties for drug possession and directed money to addiction treatment ([HB 4002 staff measure summary](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2024R1/Downloads/MeasureAnalysisDocument/82503)). It classified possession of small amounts of controlled substances as a Class E violation subject to a $100 fine, which was waived if the person completed an assessment ([SB 755 staff measure summary, 2021](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2021R1/Downloads/MeasureAnalysisDocument/62427)). A violation is not a crime.

[House Bill 4002](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2024R1/Measures/Overview/HB4002) reversed that. Its [enrolled text](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2024R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/HB4002/Enrolled) repealed the Class E violation, made possession a drug enforcement misdemeanor, and set out a new sentencing scheme. It also encouraged police and prosecutors to steer cases to treatment, created a grant program for county and tribal deflection programs, changed the definition of "delivery," and raised presumptive sentences for some sales near treatment facilities, shelters and parks.

## When did HB 4002 take effect?

HB 4002 has two key dates, and the difference matters.

| Date              | What happened                                                                      |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| November 2020     | Voters pass Measure 110                                                            |
| April 1, 2024     | Governor signs HB 4002; the bill takes effect on passage                           |
| September 1, 2024 | The possession changes become operative and apply to conduct on or after this date |
| July 17, 2025     | SB 236 takes effect, including a separate fentanyl possession statute              |

Sources: the [HB 4002 overview and history](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2024R1/Measures/Overview/HB4002), sections 73 and 74 of the enrolled bill, and the [SB 236 overview and history](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2025R1/Measures/Overview/SB236).

Section 74 of HB 4002 says the possession changes apply to conduct "occurring, or alleged to have occurred, on or after September 1, 2024." If the alleged possession happened before that date, the drug enforcement misdemeanor does not apply to it.

## What is a drug enforcement misdemeanor?

It is the level of crime that now applies to knowingly possessing a small amount of a controlled substance without a valid prescription. Under [ORS 475.752](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors475.html#:~:text=475.752%20Prohibited%20acts%20generally%3B%20penalties%3B%20exceptions%3B), possession of a Schedule I, II, III or IV controlled substance is a drug enforcement misdemeanor, unless a drug-specific statute or a quantity rule makes it more serious. Possession of a Schedule V substance is a violation. Because that statute does not give it a class, it is treated as a Class B violation ([ORS 153.015](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.015)); see our guide to [Oregon violation classes and fines](/blog/oregon-traffic-violation-classes-fines/).

Larger amounts are charged at higher levels:

| Drug                                                                            | Drug enforcement misdemeanor                 | Class A misdemeanor                     | Felony                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Heroin ([ORS 475.854](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_475.854))          | Less than 1 gram                             | 1 gram or more                          | Class B, if commercial or a substantial quantity |
| MDMA ([ORS 475.874](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors475.html#:~:text=475.874%20Unlawful%20possession%20of%203%2C4%2Dmethylenedioxymethamphetamine.))            | Less than 1 gram and fewer than 5 pills      | 1 gram or more, or 5 or more pills      | Class B, if commercial or a substantial quantity |
| Cocaine ([ORS 475.884](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors475.html#:~:text=475.884%20Unlawful%20possession%20of%20cocaine.))         | Less than 2 grams                            | 2 grams or more                         | Class C, if commercial or a substantial quantity |
| Methamphetamine ([ORS 475.894](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors475.html#:~:text=475.894%20Unlawful%20possession%20of%20methamphetamine.)) | Less than 2 grams                            | 2 grams or more                         | Class C, if commercial or a substantial quantity |
| Fentanyl (ORS 475.794)                                                          | Less than 1 gram and fewer than 5 user units | 1 gram or more, or 5 or more user units | Class C, if commercial or a substantial quantity |

"Commercial drug offense" and "substantial quantity" are defined in [ORS 475.900](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors475.html#:~:text=475.900%20Crime%20category%20classification%3B%20proof%20of%20commercial%20drug%20offense.). A commercial drug offense requires at least three listed factors, such as $300 or more in cash, packaging materials or customer lists.

## What is the sentence for a drug enforcement misdemeanor?

Under ORS 475.896 ([ORS chapter 475](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors475.html)):

- **Probation is the default.** The court must suspend any jail sentence and impose supervised probation of up to 18 months.
- **Jail only at your request.** A court may impose up to 180 days of jail instead of probation only if the defendant asks for it.
- **No jail as a probation condition**, and no fines, costs, assessments or attorney fees in the judgment.
- **Sanctions for violations are capped.** Jail sanctions for probation violations may total no more than 30 days, with early release to treatment allowed.
- **Revocation.** If probation is revoked, the court may impose up to 180 days, with early release to a drug and alcohol treatment program when one is available.

## What is deflection?

A deflection program is a partnership between law enforcement and behavioral health or social service organizations that connects people with possible substance use disorders to treatment, recovery support, housing and other services (ORS 137.687, in [ORS chapter 137](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors137.html)).

Oregon law encourages, but does not require, police to refer a person to a deflection program instead of citing or arresting them, or after a citation but before the case goes to the district attorney. District attorneys are also encouraged to divert these cases to treatment (ORS 133.042, in [ORS chapter 133](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors133.html)). Programs are run locally, funded by state grants to counties and tribal governments (ORS 430.430, in [ORS chapter 430](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors430.html)), so what is offered can differ by place. The Oregon Criminal Justice Commission keeps a public list of deflection programs (ORS 137.687).

If you complete a deflection program after a referral for a drug enforcement misdemeanor, police and prosecutors must seal the related records within 60 days of verification. Records of a citation are also sealed if two years pass with no further prosecution (ORS 137.224).

## What is conditional discharge under HB 4002?

Conditional discharge is a probation agreement that can end with the possession charge dismissed. Under ORS 475.897:

- **You do not plead guilty.** The agreement cannot require a guilty or no contest plea.
- **It lasts 12 months**, subject to early termination, and requires a substance abuse evaluation and any recommended treatment.
- **You waive trial rights.** These include a jury trial and the right to contest the evidence. If you violate the agreement, the court may impose sanctions of up to 30 days in total or resume the case, and you cannot then contest the sufficiency of the evidence.
- **Completion means dismissal.** The charge is dismissed with prejudice and is not a conviction. The court must then order the records sealed (ORS 137.224).
- **The prosecutor can object.** The court may deny entry if it would not serve the person's needs or the community's protection and welfare.

> **Deadline:** A request to enter conditional discharge must be made no later than 30 days after your first appearance, unless the court allows a later date for good cause. Filing a demurrer, a motion to suppress or a motion for an omnibus hearing does not count as good cause (ORS 475.897).

## Did the Legislature change HB 4002 in 2025?

Yes. [Senate Bill 236](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2025R1/Measures/Overview/SB236) took effect July 17, 2025. It:

- Moved fentanyl possession, delivery and manufacture into separate statutes. Fentanyl possession is now in ORS 475.794, with the thresholds shown in the table above.
- Changed conditional discharge procedures, including what a court may do when the probation period ends without notice that the terms were met.
- Changed when deflection coordinators give notice of completion, and the procedures for sealing drug enforcement misdemeanor records.

This post reflects the 2025 edition of the Oregon Revised Statutes, which includes SB 236. Laws change, so confirm the current text before relying on it.

## Can you get in trouble for calling 911 about an overdose?

Oregon law protects people who call for help. A person who contacts emergency services or police to get help for someone having a drug-related overdose, and the person who needs help, are immune from arrest and prosecution for possession and certain related offenses if the evidence was found because of the call ([ORS 475.898](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_475.898)). The protection does not cover every crime.

## What should you do if you are charged?

- Note the date of the alleged possession. Before or after September 1, 2024, changes the rules.
- Note your first appearance date and count 30 days for the conditional discharge deadline.
- Ask whether a deflection referral was made, or is still possible.
- Keep every court date. A warrant can delay sealing (ORS 137.224).
