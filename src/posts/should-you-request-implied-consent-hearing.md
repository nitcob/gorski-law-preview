Should I request an implied consent hearing? In Oregon, this hearing is how you contest a DMV suspension for refusing a test or failing a breath or blood test before it takes effect. If DMV does not receive your request within 10 days of the arrest, the suspension starts on schedule ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410)). Whether a hearing is worth it depends on your facts, so it helps to know exactly what it can and cannot do.

DUII is Oregon's legal term for what most states call DUI: driving under the influence of intoxicants ([ORS 813.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.010)). This post explains the hearing's limits, how it runs and how it relates to the criminal case. It is general information, not advice about your situation.

## What is an implied consent hearing?

It is an administrative hearing about your license, not a trial. It is held by an administrative law judge from Oregon's Office of Administrative Hearings, and DMV may authorize that judge to issue the final order ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410)). DMV's [implied consent hearing page](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/dmv/pages/driverid/ichearing.aspx) describes the purpose simply: the judge decides whether the suspension is valid. DMV also notes that this is not the same thing as an "administrative review" or an "administrative hearing," which are different DMV processes.

The officer's rights-and-consequences information tells you about this right. It says you may challenge the suspension at a hearing before it takes effect, that you must ask in writing, and that if you win, your driving privileges will not be suspended ([ORS 813.130](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.130)).

## What can the judge decide?

Very little, by design. ORS 813.410(8) says its scope "shall be narrowly construed" and is limited to whether the suspension is valid. A suspension is valid if all of these were met:

1. When asked to take the test, you were under arrest for DUII.
2. Police had reasonable grounds to believe you had been driving under the influence.
3. You refused a test, or a breath or blood test showed 0.08 percent or more (0.04 percent in a commercial motor vehicle, any amount if you are under 21).
4. If the report says you were driving a commercial motor vehicle, it actually was one.
5. You were informed of your rights and consequences as ORS 813.130 requires.
6. You were given the required written notice.
7. If you took a test, the person giving it was qualified ([ORS 813.160](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.160)).
8. If you took a test, the methods, procedures and equipment met ORS 813.160.

## What does the hearing decide, and what doesn't it?

| Question                                                                   | Decided at the hearing?                           | Where the law says so   |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Were you under arrest for DUII when asked to test?                         | Yes                                               | ORS 813.410(8)(a)       |
| Did police have reasonable grounds?                                        | Yes                                               | ORS 813.410(8)(b)       |
| Did you refuse, or did the test meet the alcohol level?                    | Yes                                               | ORS 813.410(8)(c)       |
| Were you given the rights-and-consequences information and written notice? | Yes                                               | ORS 813.410(8)(e)–(f)   |
| Was the tester qualified, and were the test methods and equipment proper?  | Yes                                               | ORS 813.410(8)(g)–(h)   |
| Are you guilty of DUII?                                                    | No. That is the criminal case                     | ORS 813.410(8); 813.010 |
| Fines, jail or treatment on a DUII conviction                              | No. The criminal court decides                    | ORS 813.010, [813.020](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.020) |
| Whether you get diversion                                                  | No. The criminal court decides                    | [ORS 813.220](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.220) |
| The separate suspension after a DUII conviction                            | No. DMV imposes it after conviction               | [ORS 813.400](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.400) |
| Whether you can get a hardship permit                                      | No. Separate process with its own waiting periods | [ORS 813.520](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.520) |

The point of the table: a hearing is a focused check on how the arrest, the test request and the test were handled. It is not a trial of the whole case.

## How does the hearing work?

- **Request it in writing, on time.** DMV must receive the request within 10 days of arrest. For a failed blood test, it is 10 days from the date DMV sends its notice. You can request it online, by fax or by mail, but not by phone (DMV). Our post on [what to do after a DUI arrest in Oregon](/blog/what-to-do-after-duii-arrest-oregon/) lists exactly what the request must include.
- **By phone unless someone asks otherwise.** The judge holds the hearing by telephone or another two-way electronic device. You or the police officer may ask for an in-person hearing, following DMV's rules on how and when. An in-person hearing is held in the county where the offense allegedly happened, or within 100 miles of it, unless you and DMV agree otherwise.
- **Witnesses and documents.** The statute provides for oaths and for subpoenas for witnesses and relevant documents. The hearing is recorded.
- **You must appear.** If you fail to appear, personally or through a lawyer, without just cause, you waive the hearing and DMV is not required to make any showing. You also lose the right to appeal. DMV's page explains how to ask for "relief from default" if circumstances beyond your reasonable control caused you to miss it.
- **Fast timing.** DMV must hold the hearing and issue a final order within 30 days of the arrest, or within 60 days of receiving a failed blood test report. Limited exceptions allow rescheduling, such as a subpoenaed officer's illness or a lawyer's scheduling conflict ([ORS 813.440](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.440)).

All of these points come from ORS 813.410 unless another source is linked.

## What happens if you win or lose?

If you win, your driving privileges are not suspended on that report ([ORS 813.130](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.130)).

If you lose, the suspension takes effect and stays in effect during any appeal; there is no stay ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410)). You can appeal by filing a petition in the circuit court for the county where you live (or, if you live outside Oregon, the county where you were arrested) within 30 days after the final order. The court reviews the case without a jury, and the review is limited to the record of the DMV hearing ([ORS 813.450](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.450)). Because an appeal looks only at that record, what gets said and offered at the hearing matters.

## How does the hearing relate to the criminal case?

They are separate. The hearing does not decide guilt, and the criminal case does not decide the suspension: the rights-and-consequences information says the outcome of a criminal DUII charge will not affect the suspension ([ORS 813.130](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.130)).

They do overlap in the facts. Because witnesses can be subpoenaed and the hearing is recorded, it may be an early chance to hear how the officer describes the stop and the test. Whether that helps, and how testimony at the hearing could matter later, depends on the case. That is a question to raise with your lawyer before the hearing.

## What happens if you don't request a hearing?

The suspension begins on the 30th day after arrest, or on the 60th day after DMV receives a failed blood test report ([ORS 813.410](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.410)). Its length is set by statute ([ORS 813.420](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.420)):

- 90 days for a failed test, or 1 year with certain history in the past five years.
- 1 year for a refusal, or 3 years with that history.

The history that triggers the longer periods includes being in a DUII diversion program now, or, in the five years before the arrest, a prior implied consent suspension, a DUII conviction or starting a diversion program ([ORS 813.430](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.430)). Your temporary permit, if you got one, ends on day 30 ([ORS 813.110](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.110)). Refusals carry longer waits before a hardship permit is possible; our post on [refusing a breath test in Oregon](/blog/refusing-breath-test-oregon-suspension-hardship-permit/) covers those rules.

If you miss the deadline, DMV can hold a late hearing only for reasons such as a physician-verified incapacity, a death in your immediate family, DMV error or other just cause defined by DMV rule ([ORS 813.440](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.440)).

## What should you weigh before deciding?

These are the questions to bring to a lawyer before day 10:

- Did you refuse, fail a breath test or fail a blood test? The deadline and suspension start date differ.
- What is your exact 10-day date, and does it fall on a weekend or holiday? DMV treats weekend and holiday arrivals as received the next business day.
- Do you hold a CDL? ORS 813.410 also provides for suspending commercial driving privileges. See our page for [commercial drivers](/traffic-tickets/cdl-commercial-drivers/).
- Do you have history that could lengthen the suspension under ORS 813.430?
- Is any of the eight requirements in doubt, such as the written notice or the test procedure?
- Can you or your lawyer attend at the scheduled time? Missing the hearing without just cause waives it.
