Oregon has no legal THC limit for driving. The only number in Oregon's DUII statute is for alcohol, 0.08 percent ([ORS 813.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.010)). So how is marijuana impairment proven in Oregon? The state has to show you were "under the influence" while driving, using evidence such as your driving, what the officer saw, field sobriety tests, a drug evaluation and a urine or blood test.

DUII is Oregon's legal term for what most states call DUI: driving under the influence of intoxicants. If the two terms confuse you, our post on [DUI vs. DUII in Oregon](/blog/dui-vs-duii-oregon/) explains the difference.

## Is there a legal THC limit for driving in Oregon?

No. Under ORS 813.010, a person commits DUII by driving while the person:

1. has 0.08 percent or more alcohol in the blood, shown by a breath or blood test;
2. "is under the influence of an intoxicant or a combination of intoxicants"; or
3. within two hours after driving, without drinking in between, has 0.08 percent or more alcohol in the blood.

Only the alcohol routes use a number. A marijuana case goes under the second route. That means a test result showing cannabis does not, on its own, meet the statute's definition. The state still has to prove you were under the influence while driving. In a criminal case it must prove that beyond a reasonable doubt ([ORS 136.415](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_136.415)).

## What counts as an "intoxicant" in Oregon?

A 2023 law, [HB 2316](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2023R1/Measures/Overview/HB2316), added a definition of "intoxicant" to Oregon's vehicle code. It now means intoxicating liquor, a controlled substance, an inhalant, cannabis, psilocybin, or any drug that, alone or combined with those, "adversely affects a person's mental or physical faculties to a noticeable or perceptible degree" ([ORS 801.321](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.321)).

Two things follow from that list:

- **Cannabis is named outright.** Whether you bought it legally does not take it outside the DUII law.
- **Prescription and over-the-counter drugs can qualify** if they affect you to a noticeable or perceptible degree.

## How do police build a marijuana or drug DUII case?

With no number to point to, a drug DUII case is usually built from several kinds of evidence. Here is what each one is and where the rules come from.

| Evidence                    | What it is                                                                                  | Where the rules are           |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Driving and the stop        | The driving that led to the stop, and what the officer reports seeing, hearing and smelling | The police report             |
| Field sobriety tests        | Physical or mental tests approved by Oregon State Police rule to screen for impairment      | [ORS 801.272](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.272), 813.135, 813.136 |
| Breath test                 | Measures alcohol. A result under 0.08 can lead to a urine test request                      | [ORS 813.100](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.100), [ORS 813.131](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.131)          |
| Drug recognition evaluation | An evaluation by an officer trained through the Oregon State Police drug evaluation program | Oregon State Police program   |
| Urine test                  | A lab test for intoxicants other than alcohol, requested after arrest in set situations     | ORS 813.131, 813.132          |
| Blood test                  | With consent, with a warrant, or in limited cases when a person cannot consent              | ORS 813.100, 813.140          |

### Field sobriety tests

Oregon defines field sobriety tests as physical or mental tests, approved by Oregon State Police rule, that help an officer "screen for or detect probable impairment from an intoxicant" ([ORS 801.272](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_801.272)). If an officer reasonably suspects DUII, you are deemed to have consented to them ([ORS 813.135](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.135)). If you refuse, the officer will ask you to physically cooperate, and a refusal to physically submit can be used as evidence ([ORS 813.136](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.136)).

### Breath tests

The breath test under Oregon's implied consent law is for "determining the alcoholic content" of a person's blood ([ORS 813.100](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.100)). It does not measure THC. If the result is under 0.08 percent, that opens the door to a urine test request under ORS 813.131.

### Drug recognition evaluations

Oregon State Police coordinates a Drug Evaluation and Classification Program that trains officers as Drug Recognition Experts, or DREs ([Oregon State Police](https://www.oregon.gov/osp/programs/Pages/PSD.aspx)). According to the program's [school application](https://www.oregon.gov/osp/programs/SiteAssets/Lists/PSD_Accordion/EditForm/DRE%20School%20Application.pdf), training covers physiology, vital signs, standardized field sobriety testing and seven categories of drugs. The same document says DREs are "frequently called upon to differentiate between drug influence and medical and/or mental disorders." In other words, the program itself recognizes that medical and mental conditions can resemble drug influence. That is one reason the details of an evaluation matter.

### Urine tests

Under [ORS 813.131](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.131), after a DUII arrest you are deemed to consent to a urine test for intoxicants other than alcohol if either:

- your breath test showed less than 0.08 percent, or
- you were in a crash that caused injury or property damage.

The statute also sets limits:

- The officer must be certified as having at least eight hours of training in recognizing drug-impaired driving, and must reasonably suspect an intoxicant other than alcohol.
- You must be given privacy. An officer may not watch you provide the sample.
- For the result to be valid, the analysis must be done in an accredited or licensed toxicology lab.

Refusing a requested urine test is treated as a breath test refusal for suspension purposes, and that suspension runs after any other implied consent suspension ([ORS 813.132](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.132)). It also carries the refusal fine, a $650 presumptive fine ([ORS 813.095](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.095)). There is an exception if an Oregon-licensed physician documents a medical condition that makes giving a sample impossible. Our post on [refusing a test and the license suspension that follows](/blog/refusing-breath-test-oregon-suspension-hardship-permit/) covers the suspension lengths.

### Blood tests

Police may test blood or urine for cannabis and other intoxicants if you expressly consent. They may also test without consent if they have probable cause and you are unconscious or otherwise unable to consent ([ORS 813.140](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.140)). And police can get a test "through any lawful means," including a search warrant (ORS 813.100).

## Does a positive drug test prove impairment?

Not by itself. Because Oregon sets no THC number, the question is whether you were under the influence while driving. The urine test statute describes a valid result as evidence that "may be used with other evidence, if any, to determine whether the person was driving while under the influence of intoxicants" (ORS 813.131).

The same idea applies when alcohol and cannabis are both involved. DUII covers "a combination of intoxicants" (ORS 813.010). An alcohol result under 0.08 is not a safe harbor either. It is "indirect evidence that may be used with other evidence" ([ORS 813.300](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.300)).

## Can prescription drugs lead to a DUII?

Yes, if they impair you. Any drug that adversely affects your mental or physical faculties to a noticeable or perceptible degree is an intoxicant (ORS 801.321).

There is a narrow affirmative defense in [ORS 813.015](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_813.015). It may apply if both of these are true:

- you got the drug by prescription and took it as prescribed, following all directions and warnings from the manufacturer, pharmacist and prescriber, or you took an over-the-counter drug at the recommended dose and followed all directions and warnings; and
- you had a reaction you could not reasonably have anticipated.

The defense has limits. Written notice must be filed with the court at least 45 days before the first trial date, unless the parties agree otherwise or the court allows a later date for good cause, and never less than 21 days before trial. The defense is not available when the charge involves alcohol, a controlled substance, an inhalant, cannabis or psilocybin. Oregon defines "controlled substance" by the federal drug schedules ([ORS 475.005](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_475.005)), and some prescription medications are on those schedules. Whether the defense fits depends on the specific drug.

## Is legal cannabis a defense to DUII?

No. Cannabis is listed by name as an intoxicant (ORS 801.321), and the affirmative defense in ORS 813.015 expressly does not apply in cannabis cases. Nothing in ORS 813.010 or 813.015 creates an exception for cannabis that was bought legally or used for medical reasons. The question is still whether you were impaired while driving.

DUII is a Class A misdemeanor in most cases, and a first conviction carries a minimum fine of $1,000 (ORS 813.010). Those base penalties apply whether the intoxicant is alcohol, cannabis or another drug.

## What questions might a defense lawyer look at?

Every case is different, and none of these guarantees any result. A lawyer reviewing a marijuana or drug DUII will often look at questions like these:

- What driving, if any, suggested impairment before the stop?
- Which field sobriety tests were given, and were they the tests approved under ORS 801.272?
- Did the officer who requested a urine test have the required training and reasonable suspicion?
- Was the urine sample collected in private and analyzed by an accredited or licensed lab?
- If a drug recognition evaluation was done, what did it record, and could a medical condition explain it?
- If the charge rests on a controlled substance, was that pleaded in the charging document as ORS 813.010 requires?
