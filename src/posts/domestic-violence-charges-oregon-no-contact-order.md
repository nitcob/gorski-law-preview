The short answer about a no contact order: Oregon domestic violence charges almost always come with one. While a person charged with a domestic violence crime is in jail, Oregon law bars them from contacting the alleged victim directly or through someone else ([ORS 135.247](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.247)). When the court releases them, usually at the first court date, the release agreement must include a condition that they not contact the alleged victim ([ORS 135.250](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.250)).

Only a judge can change that condition. Police must arrest someone they have probable cause to believe has broken it ([ORS 133.310](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_133.310)).

## Why do police have to make an arrest in a domestic disturbance?

Oregon has a mandatory arrest rule. When an officer responds to a domestic disturbance and has probable cause to believe that an assault happened between family or household members, or that one placed the other in fear of imminent serious physical injury, the officer must arrest the person the officer believes is the assailant ([ORS 133.055](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_133.055)). For this rule, "assault" includes strangulation.

The officer is not required to arrest both people. The officer must try to identify the assailant by weighing the injuries or threats on each side, any known history of domestic violence, whether anyone acted in self-defense, and the risk of future assaults. When an officer has reason to believe a family or household member has been abused, the officer must also hand each person a written notice of the legal rights and remedies available, including restraining orders.

## Who counts as a family or household member?

For release decisions, "domestic violence" means abuse between family or household members ([ORS 135.230](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.230)). That group is broader than many people expect:

- Spouses and former spouses.
- Adults related by blood or marriage.
- People who live together or have lived together.
- People who have been in a sexually intimate relationship.
- Unmarried parents of a minor child.

## Can you contact the alleged victim while you are in jail?

No. When a release assistance officer makes a release decision for someone charged with a crime that constitutes domestic violence, the decision must include an order barring contact with the alleged victim, directly or through a third party, while the person is in custody. The court continues that order at arraignment. It applies any time the person is in custody on the charge and remains valid until sentencing, dismissal or acquittal, unless a court ends it after a hearing on the alleged victim's petition (ORS 135.247).

## When is the first court date, and what happens there?

The first court date is usually the arraignment. If you are in custody, Oregon law says the arraignment must be held within the first 36 hours of custody, not counting Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, unless there is good cause or you ask for a delay ([ORS 135.010](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.010)).

At the arraignment, generally:

- **You hear the charge.** You must appear in person on a felony charge. On a misdemeanor, you may appear in person or through a lawyer ([ORS 135.030](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.030)).
- **You are told about your right to a lawyer.** If you come without one, the court must tell you that you have the right to counsel before being arraigned and ask if you want one ([ORS 135.040](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.040)).
- **The judge makes a release decision.** This normally happens at the arraignment or other first appearance. It can be postponed for good cause, such as when the district attorney plans to seek detention, but the release hearing then generally must be held within 48 hours ([ORS 135.245](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.245)).
- **The alleged victim may speak.** The district attorney must make reasonable efforts to tell the alleged victim about the arraignment, and if present, the alleged victim may express views relevant to the issues (ORS 135.245).

The law tells the judge to choose the least onerous release conditions reasonably likely to protect the public and the alleged victim and to ensure you come back to court. In a domestic violence case, the conditions must also be aimed at ensuring you do not engage in domestic violence while released (ORS 135.245). The no contact condition is part of that.

## What does the no contact release condition mean?

If you are charged with an offense that also constitutes domestic violence, the court must make "no contact with the victim" a condition of release (ORS 135.250). A separate statute adds that any pretrial release order must prohibit any contact, direct or indirect, unless the court with the criminal case specifically allows it ([ORS 135.970](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.970)).

Unless a judge's order says otherwise, treat all of these as contact:

- Calls, texts, emails, letters and voicemails.
- Messages on social media, including comments, likes and tags.
- Messages passed through friends, family or co-workers.
- Showing up where the person lives or works.
- Replying if the alleged victim reaches out first.

The condition can be waived only if the alleged victim petitions the court and the judge finds, after a hearing, that waiving it is in the best interests of the parties and the community (ORS 135.250). Even if the alleged victim wants contact, the order stands until a judge changes it.

The order does not stop your defense lawyer from contacting the alleged victim in the way the law allows. The alleged victim does not have to talk to the defense (ORS 135.970).

## What happens if the no contact condition is violated?

The consequences can stack up:

- **Arrest.** A police officer must arrest a person who is on release on a charge and who the officer has probable cause to believe has failed to comply with a no contact condition (ORS 133.310).
- **A warrant.** The court may issue a warrant for the arrest of a person who fails to follow any release condition ([ORS 135.280](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.280)).
- **Contempt of court.** Knowingly breaking a release regulation, or the in-custody no contact order, can be punished as contempt ([ORS 135.290](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_135.290)).
- **Loss of release.** If the court finds that the defendant threatened or intimidated the alleged victim, directly or indirectly, release must be revoked (ORS 135.970).

## What is Assault IV, and when is it a felony?

Many domestic violence cases are charged as assault in the fourth degree. Under [ORS 163.160](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors163.html#:~:text=163.160%20Assault%20in%20the%20fourth%20degree.), that includes intentionally, knowingly or recklessly causing physical injury to another person. It is a Class A misdemeanor. It becomes a Class C felony in listed situations, including when:

- The assault happens in the immediate presence of, or is seen or directly perceived by, a minor child or stepchild of either person or a minor child living in the household.
- The person has a prior conviction for a listed crime, such as assault or strangulation, involving the same victim.
- The person has three or more prior convictions for listed assault crimes.
- The person knows the victim is pregnant.

| Charge level        | Maximum jail or prison | Maximum fine |
| ------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------ |
| Class A misdemeanor | 364 days               | $6,250       |
| Class C felony      | 5 years                | $125,000     |

Sources: [ORS 161.615](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.615), [ORS 161.635](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.635), [ORS 161.605](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.605) and [ORS 161.625](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_161.625). These are maximums, not predictions. If someone is convicted of misdemeanor Assault IV with domestic violence pleaded as part of the charge, the court must make sure the judgment shows that it constitutes domestic violence (ORS 163.160). ## Is a FAPA restraining order the same thing?

No. A Family Abuse Prevention Act (FAPA) restraining order is a separate civil case that the alleged victim starts. It can exist alongside a criminal no contact condition, and each has its own rules.

| Topic | Criminal no contact condition                      | FAPA restraining order                                                                                                                                                           |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who starts it     | The court, as part of a criminal case              | The person asking for protection files a petition                                                                                                                                |
| Basis             | A charge that constitutes domestic violence        | Abuse within the last 180 days and imminent danger of more ([ORS 107.710](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_107.710))                                                       |
| First hearing     | Arraignment or release hearing                     | An ex parte hearing the day of filing or the next judicial day ([ORS 107.718](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_107.718))                                                   |
| How long it lasts | Until the criminal case ends or a judge changes it | Up to two years unless ended sooner (ORS 107.718)                                                                                                                                |
| How to contest it | Through the criminal case                          | The respondent may request a hearing within 30 days after being served; it is held within 21 days of the request ([ORS 107.716](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_107.716)) |
| If violated       | Mandatory arrest                                   | Mandatory arrest (ORS 133.310)                                                                                                                                                   |

If no hearing is requested within 30 days of service, the FAPA order is confirmed by operation of law (ORS 107.718). The Oregon Judicial Department has [FAPA forms and instructions](https://www.courts.oregon.gov/programs/family/domestic-violence/Pages/restraining.aspx), including for people who want to challenge an order.

## Can a domestic violence case affect your right to have firearms?

It can. Under [ORS 166.255](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_166.255), it is unlawful to knowingly possess a firearm or ammunition if you are subject to certain court orders that restrain you from stalking, intimidating, molesting or menacing a family or household member, issued after notice and a chance to be heard and including a finding that you are a credible threat. The same statute applies after a conviction for a misdemeanor that has the use or attempted use of physical force as an element, when the victim was a family or household member. A domestic violence release agreement may also include firearm terms (ORS 135.250). Whether any of this applies to you depends on the exact order and charge, so ask a lawyer before you assume either way.

## What should you do before your first court date?

- Read your release agreement, and keep a copy with you.
- Follow every condition, including no contact, exactly as written.
- Write down your court date and time, and plan to be there early.
- If you need belongings from a shared home, ask your lawyer how to handle it without breaking the order.
- Do not discuss the facts of the case with anyone but your lawyer.
