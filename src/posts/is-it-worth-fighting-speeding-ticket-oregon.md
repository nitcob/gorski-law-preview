Is it worth fighting a speeding ticket in Oregon? Sometimes. It depends less on the fine than on what a conviction does to your driving record, your job and your time. For a low-level ticket on a clean record, paying with a fine reduction may cost the least. For CDL holders, drivers with other recent tickets, or very high speeds, fighting may deserve a closer look, because "only a trial verdict of not guilty or a dismissal of the violation by a judge will keep the violation off your driving record" ([Oregon Judicial Department](https://www.courts.oregon.gov/services/online/Pages/Traffic-Options.aspx)).

This post lays out the costs on both sides, so you can weigh them for your own situation.

## Is a speeding ticket a crime in Oregon?

No. Ordinary speeding is a traffic violation, not a crime. A violation is punished by a fine and cannot carry jail time ([ORS 153.008](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.008), [ORS 153.018](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors153.html#:~:text=153.018%20Maximum%20fines.)). A violation conviction also "does not give rise to any disability or legal disadvantage based on conviction of a crime" (ORS 153.008).

That does not make it nothing. A conviction still goes on your driving record. It can count toward DMV programs and, if you hold a CDL, toward federal disqualification rules.

## How much is a speeding ticket in Oregon?

The class depends on how far over the limit you were and on the type of road ([ORS 811.109](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_811.109)).

| Speed over the limit | Most roads | Roads posted at 65 mph or higher |
| -------------------- | ---------- | -------------------------------- |
| 1–10 mph             | Class D    | Class C                          |
| 11–20 mph            | Class C    | Class B                          |
| 21–30 mph            | Class B    | Class A                          |
| Over 30 mph          | Class A    | Class A                          |

The class then sets the fine. Oregon law sets a presumptive fine ([ORS 153.019](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors153.html#:~:text=153.019%20Presumptive%20fines%3B%20generally.)), a minimum fine that a court may waive if requiring it would be "inconsistent with justice in the case" ([ORS 153.021](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors153.html#:~:text=153.021%20Minimum%20fines%3B%20waiver%3B%20audit%20of%20court.)), a maximum fine (ORS 153.018), and a higher presumptive fine for work zones, school zones and safety corridors ([ORS 153.020](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors153.html#:~:text=153.020%20Presumptive%20fines%3B%20highway%20work%20zones%2C%20school%20zones%20and%20safety%20corridors.)).

| Class | Presumptive fine | Minimum fine | Maximum fine | Presumptive fine in a work zone, school zone or safety corridor |
| ----- | ---------------- | ------------ | ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A     | $440             | $225         | $2,000       | $875                                      |
| B     | $265             | $135         | $1,000       | $525                                      |
| C     | $165             | $85          | $500         | $325                                      |
| D     | $115             | $65          | $250         | $225                                      |

The presumptive fine is the amount generally set out on the citation, and any surcharge the law requires is added to it (ORS 153.019, [ORS 153.061](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors153.html#:~:text=153.061%20Appearance%20by%20defendant.)). The work zone, school zone and safety corridor amounts apply when the officer notes that on the citation.

Two speed rules can also bring a suspension:

- **100 mph or more** is a specific fine violation with a presumptive fine of $1,150. On conviction, the court must suspend your driving privileges for 30 to 90 days (ORS 811.109).
- **More than 30 mph over,** with a prior speeding conviction within 12 months before the new offense, allows the court to suspend your driving privileges for up to 30 days (ORS 811.109).

For a wider look at how Oregon classifies violations, see our post on [Oregon traffic violation classes and fines](/blog/oregon-traffic-violation-classes-fines/).

## What happens if you fight a speeding ticket?

Fighting a ticket means pleading not guilty and asking for a trial. Here is how that works under Oregon law.

1. **Ask for a trial before the "appear by" date.** You can go to court on or before the date on the citation, or send a written request for a trial before then ([ORS 153.061](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors153.html#:~:text=153.061%20Appearance%20by%20defendant.), Oregon Judicial Department).
2. **A judge decides, not a jury.** The state must prove the violation by a preponderance of the evidence, meaning more likely than not. You cannot be required to testify, and the pretrial discovery rules apply ([ORS 153.076](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.076)).
3. **Often the officer presents the case.** If no prosecutor appears, the officer who wrote the ticket may present evidence, question witnesses and make arguments ([ORS 153.083](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.083)). A district attorney or city attorney generally does not appear unless the driver has a lawyer (ORS 153.076).
4. **No public defender.** The state does not provide a lawyer at public expense when only violations are charged (ORS 153.076).
5. **Trial by written statement may be possible.** Some courts allow testimony by affidavit or declaration instead of live testimony, if you sign a waiver ([ORS 153.080](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.080)). The Oregon Judicial Department says to contact the court to find out whether you qualify.

There is a cost to losing. The Oregon Judicial Department warns that after a trial the judge "could impose up to the maximum fine for the violation, which may be higher than the amount on your original ticket." It also says a judge cannot reduce the fine by more than 50 percent.

> **Warning:** Do not ignore a ticket. If you do not appear, the court may enter a default judgment ([ORS 153.102](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_153.102)), and your driving privileges become subject to suspension (ORS 153.061).

## What are your options, side by side?

| Option                            | What happens                                                            | Does it go on your record?                     |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Plead no contest and pay          | You pay the presumptive fine, or a reduced amount if you qualify        | Yes                                            |
| Pay with a letter of explanation  | You send the full fine with a written statement about the circumstances | Yes                                            |
| Plead not guilty and go to trial  | A judge hears the evidence and decides                                  | Only kept off if found not guilty or dismissed |
| Trial by affidavit or declaration | Written statements replace live testimony, where the court allows it    | Only kept off if found not guilty or dismissed |
| Do nothing                        | Default judgment is possible, and your license is subject to suspension | Yes                                            |

Sources: Oregon Judicial Department, ORS 153.061, ORS 153.102.

## When might fighting be worth it?

These are the situations where the cost of a conviction can outweigh the cost of a trial:

- **You hold a CDL.** In a commercial vehicle, speeding 15 mph or more over the limit is a "serious traffic violation." Two of those within three years mean a 60-day disqualification ([49 CFR 383.51](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-383.51)). For CDL purposes, paying the fine counts as a conviction ([49 CFR 383.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/section-383.5)). Our post on [how a ticket can cost a CDL driver their job](/blog/cdl-drivers-oregon-ticket-cost-your-job/) covers the rest.
- **You are near DMV's Driver Improvement limits.** For drivers 18 and older, three qualifying convictions or preventable accidents in 24 months bring a 30-day restriction with no driving between midnight and 5 am. Five in 24 months bring a 30-day suspension ([Oregon DMV](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/DMV/pages/driverid/suspreasons.aspx)).
- **The speed was very high.** At 100 mph or more, a suspension is required on conviction, and the Oregon Judicial Department lists speeding over 100 mph among charges "not eligible for reduction."
- **You drive for a living.** Every conviction adds to your driving record and counts toward the DMV limits above. When driving is your job, a restriction or suspension costs more than the fine.
- **You dispute the facts.** You may disagree about the speed, the posted limit, or whether you were the driver.

## When might it not be worth it?

Fighting is not always the better choice. It may not be worth it when:

- **The ticket is minor and your record is clean.** The Oregon Judicial Department says a fine reduction may be available if you plead no contest, the violation is an eligible state traffic violation, an Oregon driver license number is on the citation, and your record shows two or fewer convictions in the last three years.
- **Your time costs more than the fine.** A trial can mean a court date, time off work and travel.
- **You could end up paying more.** After a trial, the judge can impose up to the maximum fine.
- **The evidence is strong and your record is not at risk.** If a conviction will not push you over a DMV or CDL threshold, a reduced fine may be the cheaper path.

If insurance is your main worry, ask your insurer how it treats a conviction. That depends on the company and your policy, not on Oregon traffic law.
